# Pain

What happens to training when something hurts. Fetch this whenever Marco reports
pain, a tweak, a pinch, or asks whether to train through something — before deciding
anything about the session.

This is triage, not diagnosis. The coach's job is to decide what today's training
does about a symptom, and to recognise when the question has stopped being a training
question. Naming a suspected injury, estimating recovery time for one, or overruling
a professional's advice with training logic is never the job.

## Three buckets

**Muscle soreness.** Diffuse, in the belly of a muscle that worked, roughly matching
what was trained, peaking a day or two after novel or hard work, easing as the muscle
warms up, not changing how the movement looks. This is normal and trainable. It says
the work was novel; it is not damage and not a reason to skip. Train, and let the
warmup sets confirm the read.

**Joint or tendon complaint.** Localised at or near a joint, often sharp or pinchy,
appearing in specific movements or at specific angles, sometimes warming up only to
return, lingering between sessions. This is a modify signal, not a stop-everything
signal: reduce the load or the range on the pattern that provokes it, substitute a
lift that doesn't, and if it persists across sessions, back the lift off properly —
a decision row, per `tasks/programming` ("Backing off"). Everything it doesn't touch
trains as planned.

**Stop and refer.** Any of the following ends the session's work on that area and
gets a professional involved before any workaround is programmed:

- sharp sudden pain with a pop, a tear sensation, or immediate weakness
- swelling, numbness, tingling, or pain radiating down a limb
- pain following impact or trauma
- night pain that doesn't change with position
- pain that keeps worsening across one to two weeks despite backing off
- anything systemic — chest pain, dizziness, breathlessness out of proportion to
  effort

Say plainly that this needs assessment, and don't program around the area until it
has been assessed. A professional's restrictions, once given, outrank the plan the
way spacing constraints do.

## Mid-session rules

- **A set is never finished through sharp pain.** Stop the set, whatever the target
  said. The target records what was asked; the set records what was wise.
- Mild discomfort that doesn't alter technique and doesn't build across sets can be
  trained through, watched. The moment it changes how a rep looks, or escalates set
  to set, that exercise is done for the day.
- Never train through numbness or tingling. Not once.
- When an exercise is cut, substitute a pattern that doesn't provoke — or simply do
  less. The dose is not chased on a day the body said no; a short session recorded
  honestly is information (`tasks/session-generation`, "Don't cram").

## Write it down, every time

A pain report that isn't recorded is a pattern that can't be seen. Same conversation,
per `tasks/logging`:

- **User context**, under a durable topic ("left elbow", "lower back"): what hurts,
  in which movement, at what load or angle. Reuse the topic on recurrence — the same
  elbow three times in a month is data, and it only becomes visible if the topic
  string holds still.
- **Session notes** for the incident itself: which set, what happened, what was cut.
- **A decision row** when a lift gets backed off or dropped, per "Backing off".

## Coming back

Resume the provoking pattern below where it stopped — lighter, higher reps, pain-free
range — and progress it only while it stays symptom-free during the session and the
day after. Pain that returns at the same point twice is not a loading error to be
finessed a third time; that is the refer signal.

## Two failure modes

The mistake runs in both directions. Catastrophising every ache into a lost week
kills training with caution — soreness is the commonest report there is, and it
trains. Coaching through a red flag because the plan says 12 sets is worse. The
buckets exist so neither happens: soreness trains, complaints modify, red flags
refer. When genuinely unsure which bucket a report belongs to, treat it as the more
cautious one and say why.
